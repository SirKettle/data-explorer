Six ideas, in the order you meet them. The demo at the end uses all of them.

## Focus: where the viewer is

A **focus** is a value you choose that says where the viewer is in your data: a folder path, a
database id, `{ team: "Design" }`, anything. `null` always means the top. The explorer never looks
inside a focus; it only hands it back to you.

Each focus has a **key**: a string that is equal for equal foci, used to cache and compare scenes.
`createSceneSource` makes one for you (`"root"` for `null`, the value itself for a string,
`JSON.stringify` for an object); give it `keyOf` only if your objects' key order can vary.

## Scene: what is drawn at a focus

A **scene** is everything drawn at one focus: a `heading`, the items, and any edges between them.
Your scene source builds it; the explorer lays it out and draws it. Items come in two lists:

- **`clusters`**: groups, usually drawn as soft clouds you can enter. A cluster's `href` may be
  `null`, but only for an "Other (N more)" aggregate, which also sets `truncated` (`windowed()`
  makes both for you). An aggregate is drawn hollow and cannot be entered.
- **`nodes`**: single things, drawn as discs or squares. A node always has an `href`.

Every item has an `id` (unique in its scene), a `label`, a `weight`, an `href`, a `shape`
(`"cloud"`, `"disc"` or `"square"`) and `drillsInto`. It may have `marks` (short badges, read in
the outline) and `data` (your own, handed back by the events).

**`weight` sets size, within its own list.** Nodes are sized against the other nodes in the scene,
and clusters against the other clusters; every cluster is drawn at least twice the size of the
largest node, so groups read as groups. Size grows more gently than weight (weight to the power
0.7), and the top tenth of weights is clamped, so one outlier cannot shrink everything else. Use a
real count the viewer could read (files, members, bytes), not a made-up score.

## Drilling in, zooming out

**`drillsInto`** is the focus an item opens, or `null` if there is nothing inside it. Zooming far
enough into such an item, or double-clicking it, enters it: the explorer loads the scene for that
focus and draws it inside the item, so the viewer never loses their place.

Zooming **out** asks your source's `parentOf(focus)` where to go:

| Answer | Means |
| --- | --- |
| `{ kind: "anchored", focus, anchorId }` | The parent scene has an item, `anchorId`, standing for this one; the camera zooms out of it smoothly. The usual answer for a tree. |
| `{ kind: "unanchored", focus }` | There is a parent, but no single item in it stands for this scene; the explorer swaps to it directly. Use it for a node in a cycle, or with many parents. |
| `{ kind: "none" }` | Nothing above. Also what you get without `parentOf`. |

If the viewer *entered* the scene, zooming out goes back the way they came, whatever `parentOf`
says. `parentOf` matters when they arrived another way, such as a deep link. The explorer keeps a
stack of the scenes the viewer entered and never walks your data, so a graph with cycles costs only
the scenes actually visited.

## Packed, radial and fixed scenes

A scene is laid out one of three ways:

- **Packed**: clusters and nodes packed together, biggest first. How a level of a tree is shown.
- **Radial**: one item in the middle with its neighbours round it. How one item's neighbourhood
  in a graph is shown.
- **Fixed**: every item at the rectangle you give it, for data with places of its own, such as a
  map. Set `layout: "fixed"` and give each item a `box`: see
  [Fixed layouts and regions](/data-explorer/guides/fixed-layouts/).

A scene is radial when it sets **`focusId`** to the id of the item to put in the middle. In a
radial scene:

- the **neighbours** are the items joined to the centre by an edge, in either direction. An item
  joined to the centre by no edge is **not drawn**;
- neighbours are **grouped by the `kind`** of the edge that joins them, then ordered by id, and
  placed evenly round the centre, starting at three o'clock and going clockwise;
- the **centre** is drawn at a fixed size and is never entered (it is the scene you are in), so
  its `drillsInto` is ignored; the neighbours are sized against each other;
- an edge's **direction** only matters for drawing: arrowheads point from `sourceId` to
  `targetId`. Edges between two neighbours are drawn (with edges on) but do not affect the layout.

Edges in a packed scene do not affect the layout either, but can be
[drawn](/data-explorer/guides/edges/). A neighbour can drill into its own radial scene, which is
how a viewer walks a graph.

## Loading, caching and change

While the viewer zooms towards an item, the explorer loads its scene early and fades it in inside
the item, so it needs to know what is already loaded. **`createSceneSource({ load, parentOf })`**
handles that: it calls `load` once per focus and caches the result. A failed load is not cached, so
it is retried. Its extras:

- `loadStreaming(focus, onUpdate)`: resolve with a first part quickly, then call `onUpdate` with
  fuller scenes as more arrives. Set the scene's `expectedItemCount` and the layout reserves room
  for everything from the start.
- `source.invalidate(focus?)`: when your data changes, call it for one focus or all, and any
  explorer showing the source reloads its scene in place.

If your data already lives in a cache of its own (a query library, say), implement the
[`SceneSource`](/data-explorer/reference/main/interfaces/scenesource/) interface directly instead.

## The accessible outline

The canvas is hidden from assistive technology. Beside it, the explorer renders an **outline**: the
current scene as real links (each item's `href`) and buttons, visually hidden but read aloud and
reachable by keyboard. Enter on an entry enters that item. That is why every node needs an `href`:
anything the canvas shows must also be reachable as an ordinary link. If your data has no pages of
its own, use stable in-app URLs. [Accessibility](/data-explorer/guides/accessibility/) shows it.

## All of it together

Teams are clusters at the top (packed). A team is a packed scene of its people. A person is a
radial scene: their teammates and the people they work with, grouped by the edge's `kind`.
